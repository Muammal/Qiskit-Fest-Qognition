# Audit of Test 2, 6 November 2026

`PRE_REGISTERED.md` and the scoring in `contest.py` are unchanged by this note.

## Why it was audited

In Test 2 the quantum predictor scored TVD 0.42 (0.30 with the better sign) against 0.125 for classical. That looked implausible when the QQ equality holds in 9 of 13 data sets, so a convention bug was suspected.

## What was checked

`mind/tests/test_synthetic_recovery.py` generates tables with `luders_sequential_probs`, which shares no code with `contest.py`, fits angles on the first order only, and predicts the second.

- **Exact tables:** over 300 random models, the better sign recovers the second order to a worst-case TVD of 3e-8.
- **Sampled tables, n = 450:** recovery stays within sampling noise.
- **Table conventions:** `ab_ny` is A no, B yes, and `ba_yn` is B yes, A no. The classical mapping, the angle table and `luders_sequential_probs` all agree. The published q and z values already reproduce in `test_contest.py`.

## Finding

**There is no convention bug, and nothing was fixed.**

The poor score is the model's own limit. A two-dimensional model with rank-1 projectors forces P(B yes | A yes) = P(B no | A no). The data do not obey this. For Clinton-Gore they are 0.92 and 0.62. So two angles cannot even describe the first order they are fitted to: the fit gap runs from 0.004 to 0.354, and is listed by study in `docs/HUMAN_DATA_RESULTS.md`. A predictor that starts from a bad fit of the first order then predicts the second order badly.

This is not in tension with the QQ equality. QQ follows for projectors of any rank in any dimension, so it can hold where the two-dimensional model fails. It is also only one constraint: given the first order, it does not fix the second, so no prediction of the whole second-order table can follow from QQ alone.

Averaging both signs, the pre-registered main score, also adds error from whichever sign is wrong. That choice was registered and stays.

## What this means for the claim

Test 2's result stands: as registered, the quantum predictor loses on all 13 rows, including with the better sign. The fair reading is that this two-angle predictor is too restricted to test quantum models in general. It does not show a higher-dimensional quantum model would fail, and nothing here shows one would succeed.
